How RISO Color Works

Risograph prints one ink color at a time, building up an image layer by layer. Understanding how inks interact — and how to design for it — is the key to making intentional prints.

What a Risograph Does

A Risograph is a stencil duplicator — similar in principle to a screen print or mimeograph. It burns your artwork into a drum wrapped with a master stencil, then rolls ink through it onto paper. Each ink color is a separate pass through the machine.

This means a two-color print requires two separate runs through the printer: one for each ink drum. The paper is fed through twice, and the two layers land on top of each other.

Ink is Semi-Transparent

RISO inks are not opaque — they're closer to a thin wash than a solid block of color. The paper shows through, and wherever two inks overlap, they mix optically and physically to create a third color.

The overprint color depends on both inks and isn't always predictable from the screen preview. Printing a test swatch of your chosen combination before a full run is always worth it.

Color Separation

When you design for RISO, you're creating a separate artwork for each ink color — called a color separation. Each separation is a grayscale image: black areas will print, white areas won't.

In p5.riso, each new Riso() call creates one layer. Anything you draw to that layer becomes the separation for that ink: 

let pink = new Riso("fluorescentpink"); // layer 1 — pink ink
let blue = new Riso("blue");            // layer 2 — blue ink

// Draw to pink layer:
pink.fill(255);          // full density
pink.circle(200, 300, 150);

// Draw to blue layer:
blue.fill(180);          // ~70% density — lighter print
blue.rect(100, 200, 300, 200);

The fill value on a Riso layer is ink density, not color — 255 is full coverage, 0 is none. The actual color comes from the ink drum.

Designing for Two Colors

The most interesting RISO prints use the overlap intentionally. Think of your composition in three zones:

  • Color 1 only — areas only the first ink covers
  • Color 2 only — areas only the second ink covers
  • Overprint — areas where both inks overlap, creating a third color
  • Paper — unprinted areas, which contribute the paper color to the composition

Choosing ink colors that produce an interesting overprint gives you effectively three colors from two passes. Blue + yellow = green. Pink + yellow = orange. Pink + blue = purple.

The Screen Preview is an Approximation

drawRiso() composites your layers on screen using a multiply blend mode — a reasonable simulation, but not identical to real ink on paper. Real ink is affected by paper absorbency, ink viscosity, drum pressure, and registration, none of which a screen can replicate.

Fluorescent inks are especially hard to preview accurately — they look much more vivid and saturated on paper than on screen.

Registration

Because the paper feeds through the printer twice, the two layers will never align with pixel-perfect precision. A small amount of misregistration — a 1–3mm shift — is normal and expected. It's part of the RISO aesthetic.

Design with this in mind:

  • Avoid designs where perfect alignment is critical — fine text in two colors, for example, will be blurry
  • Embrace the offset — a slight shift in layers can add energy and a handmade quality
  • Large shapes and dithered images tolerate misregistration better than thin lines or tight type

Halftone and Dither

RISO prints in a single density — each dot of ink is either fully on or fully off. To create the illusion of lighter tones or photographic images, p5.riso converts images into dot patterns before printing:

  • Dithering — scatters dots to approximate continuous tone. Atkinson dither gives a high-contrast, graphic look; Floyd-Steinberg gives finer gradients.
  • Halftone — arranges dots in a regular grid pattern, like traditional offset printing. Frequency controls dot size; angle controls the grid direction.
