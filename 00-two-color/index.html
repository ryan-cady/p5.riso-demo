Two-Color Design

How to think about composition, layer separation, and ink choice when designing a two-color RISO print.

Think in Four Zones

A two-color RISO print has four distinct zones. Good compositions use all of them intentionally:

Color 1 only
Areas printed in ink 1 alone
Color 2 only
Areas printed in ink 2 alone
Overprint
Both inks overlap — a third color emerges
Paper
Unprinted — the paper color contributes to the composition

Beginners often use only two zones (color 1 and color 2 in separate areas). The most interesting prints deliberately design the overprint and treat the paper as a fourth color.

Choosing Colors for a Good Overprint

The overprint color is produced by both inks mixing on the paper. Some combinations produce more useful third colors than others:

Ink 1 Ink 2 Overprint result
Fluor. Pink Yellow Orange
Fluor. Pink Blue Purple
Blue Yellow Green
Orange Blue Dark brown/olive
Green Fluor. Pink Muddy yellow-green

The screen preview uses a multiply blend mode — a reasonable approximation, but the real overprint will look different. Print a test swatch of your combination before running a full edition.

Ink Density and Overprint Control

The fill value on a Riso layer (0–255) controls ink density. Lower density means less ink, which lightens the layer and produces a softer overprint: 

pink.fill(255); // full density
blue.fill(150); // ~60% — lighter blue, softer overprint where they meet

Varying density across layers lets you control how prominent the overprint zone is relative to the individual color areas.

Layer Order

In p5.riso, the layer printed first goes down first — later layers print on top. This matters for overprints: whichever ink is printed second will tend to show more in the overlap area because it's laid down on top of dried ink. 

let pink = new Riso("fluorescentpink"); // printed first
let blue = new Riso("blue");            // printed second — sits on top

In practice, the RISO operator determines the actual print order. Make sure your file names and notes communicate which layer goes first.

Common Approaches

Subject / Background split

Put the main subject on one layer and a background texture or shape on the other. The overlap creates depth where the subject sits on the background.

Outline / Fill split

Line art or contours on one layer, filled shapes on the other. The overprint zone — where lines cross fills — adds a third color that defines edges.

Geometry + photography

A dithered photograph on one layer, bold geometric shapes on the other. The photo provides texture; the geometry provides structure. They create a third reading wherever they intersect.

Shifted duplicates

Draw the same or similar composition on both layers, offset slightly. The slight shift creates a shadow/echo effect, and the overlap zone darkens the image in the center of each shape.

What to Avoid

  • Fine text in two colors — misregistration will blur thin letterforms. Put text on a single layer.
  • Designs that only work with perfect alignment — a 1–3mm shift is normal. If your composition depends on exact registration, it will look wrong in print.
  • Ignoring the paper — white/unprinted areas are part of the composition. Design them deliberately rather than leaving them as empty leftover space.
  • Muddy overprint combinations — complementary colors (opposite on the color wheel) tend to produce dull brownish-green overprints. Test before committing.
